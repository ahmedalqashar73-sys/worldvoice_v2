## STORY INTRODUCTION
A second light appears near the rocks during a terrible storm. The following morning, a lighthouse keeper is found dead and his daughter has vanished. A young harbour clerk and a telegraph operator begin asking questions. To learn the truth, they must follow old records, understand the tides, and discover why a false light might be worth more than a ship.

## CHARACTERS
Ned Mercer — A careful twenty-one-year-old clerk at Dunmere Harbour, curious about numbers and records.
Maggie Rowe — The village telegraph operator, calm under pressure and skilled at reading messages.
Clara Vale — The lighthouse keeper's nineteen-year-old daughter, observant and brave.
Samuel Vale — Clara's father, keeper of the lighthouse at Blackglass Head.
Captain Amos Pike — An experienced fisherman who knows the coast and its dangerous tides.
Stephen Voss — The respected harbourmaster, who keeps the official shipping records.
Constable Henry Hale — Dunmere's local police officer, impatient with questions.
Silas Crowe — A boatman who sometimes works at the abandoned western signal station.
Dr Eleanor Calder — The village doctor, careful about evidence.
Inspector Alice Carver — A police inspector sent from Plymouth.

# CHAPTER 1 — TWO LIGHTS IN THE STORM

The wind had been growing stronger since the afternoon. By nine o'clock, the fishing boats in Dunmere Harbour were tied tightly to the wooden posts, and most of the village had closed its doors. Rain struck the windows like small stones. The narrow streets were empty except for a few men making their final journey home.

Ned Mercer was one of the few people still working. He was twenty-one years old and kept records in the harbour office. His job was to write down the names of arriving ships, check their cargo papers, and help Stephen Voss, the harbourmaster, prepare reports. Ned liked the work because numbers usually made sense, even when people did not.

That evening he had stayed late to correct a mistake in the week's list of cargo. He was closing a window when he noticed something unusual across the water. On Blackglass Head, the lighthouse shone as it always did. Its bright beam moved slowly over the dark sea.

Farther west, near a dangerous group of rocks called Raven's Point, another light appeared. It was weaker than the lighthouse beam and much lower. It vanished for a moment, then returned behind a line of black rocks.

"That cannot be right," Ned whispered.

He put on his coat and ran outside. Captain Amos Pike, an old fisherman with a grey beard, was fixing the last rope on his boat.

"Captain! Look towards Raven's Point."

Pike turned and stared into the rain. "Who would put a light there? If a ship follows it, she'll strike the rocks."

A trading ship was approaching from the west. Its sails were difficult to see through the storm. Ned ran to the harbour bell, and Pike followed. Together they pulled the heavy rope until the warning rang across the village. At the same moment, Ned saw a small dark shape move beside the second light.

"Someone is carrying it," he said.

The light went out. A few seconds later it appeared again, this time a little farther inland.

The door of the telegraph office opened. Maggie Rowe stepped into the rain, holding her hat against the wind. She worked the telegraph and knew how quickly a message could travel along the coast.

"Send a warning to the western station!" Ned shouted. "There is a false light at Raven's Point."

Maggie hurried inside. Before long the approaching ship changed direction. It moved into safer water, and the captain of the vessel sounded his horn twice.

"You may have saved those men," Pike told Ned.

But Ned continued watching Raven's Point. The light had disappeared. He wondered who had carried it and why anyone would want a ship to approach the rocks. He thought about reporting the incident immediately, but the harbour office was locked and Mr Voss had already gone home.

At dawn a messenger arrived at Ned's door. He was pale and out of breath. "There has been trouble at Blackglass Head. They have found Samuel Vale."

Ned followed him along the steep coastal road. Two policemen stood outside the lighthouse. Inside, at the bottom of its iron staircase, lay the body of Samuel Vale, the lighthouse keeper.

Dr Eleanor Calder knelt beside him. "He has been dead for several hours," she said quietly.

Constable Henry Hale looked towards the stairs. "The storm made the steps wet. He must have fallen."

Ned noticed a dark mark on Samuel's sleeve and a short piece of red thread caught in the wooden doorframe. He said nothing at first.

"Where is Clara?" he finally asked. Samuel's daughter often helped her father with the records.

No one answered.

Then Dr Calder stood up. "We cannot find her anywhere."

Outside, the sea had become calm again. The lighthouse continued shining over the water, but Ned remembered the second light moving among the rocks. Perhaps Samuel Vale had seen it too. If so, Ned feared that the missing daughter and her father's death might belong to the same terrible story.

# CHAPTER 2 — THE MISSING PAGE

By midday everyone in Dunmere had heard the news. Some villagers believed Samuel Vale had fallen because of the storm. Others whispered that Clara had run away. Neither explanation satisfied Ned. He knew Clara was close to her father. He also knew Samuel had checked every step in his lighthouse each evening.

Ned visited Maggie at the telegraph office. She was sorting messages into small wooden compartments, but she stopped when she saw his face.

"Did the western station receive our warning?" he asked.

"At ten seventeen. They answered that the ship reached safe water." She hesitated, then opened a drawer. "Clara came here yesterday afternoon. She wanted me to send a message to her aunt in Plymouth."

On the paper was written: PLEASE COME TO DUNMERE. FATHER HAS DISCOVERED SOMETHING IMPORTANT. WE ARE AFRAID.

"Was it sent?" Ned asked.

"No. Clara returned before I had finished preparing it. She asked me to stop. Someone had been watching the lighthouse, she said."

Maggie had kept the paper because the sudden change troubled her. Ned read the message again. It did not sound like a girl planning to leave home. It sounded like a warning that had arrived too late.

They visited the lighthouse with permission from Dr Calder. The keeper's desk stood beside a small window that faced the western rocks. There were maps, a brass clock, pencils, and a thick leather record book. Samuel had written in it each night for nearly twenty years: weather, passing vessels, repairs to the lamp, and anything unusual he had seen.

Ned turned the pages slowly. On 14 October the keeper had written only three strange words: RAVEN'S POINT. AGAIN. The next entry described someone entering the old signal station. Samuel had intended to speak to the harbourmaster.

One page had been torn from the book. The date on the following page was 17 October. Ned touched the rough edge. Whoever had removed the paper had been in a hurry.

"Could Clara have taken it?" Maggie asked.

"Perhaps. Or someone took it because of what Samuel had written."

Dr Calder came into the room with another piece of information. "Samuel had a wound at the back of his head. It could have been caused by a fall, but the shape is unusual. I have asked that no one move anything else."

Ned thought of the dark mark on the keeper's sleeve. There was a smell of machine oil near the door, although the lamp room was upstairs. Maggie noticed that Ned was keeping his hands away from everything.

"Good," the doctor said. "If we expect others to believe our observations, we must not spoil the evidence."

That evening Ned went to the harbour office. Stephen Voss was sitting behind his large desk, reading a letter.

"Samuel Vale wrote that he wanted to speak to you," Ned said.

"He did?" Voss looked up sharply.

"Did he come?"

"No. And I don't enjoy having my staff question me as though I am a criminal."

Voss closed the letter and pushed it beneath a ledger. Ned noticed a coil of thick rope under the desk, with one short red thread hanging from its end. The thread looked similar to the one in the lighthouse door.

Ned lowered his eyes. "Forgive me, sir. I only hoped we might help Clara."

"Leave that to the police. Tomorrow you will work as usual."

As Ned walked away, he heard Voss unlock a drawer. Ned wondered why the harbourmaster had hidden his letter so quickly. The red thread might mean nothing; fishermen used many kinds of rope. But now Ned had two questions instead of one: what had Samuel discovered at Raven's Point, and what was Stephen Voss afraid Ned might find?

# CHAPTER 3 — THE PATH ABOVE THE ROCKS

On the following morning, Captain Pike took Ned and Maggie in his fishing boat along the western coast. They left before the tide reached its highest point. Pike had sailed past Raven's Point for forty years, and he knew that a stranger might believe the water was deep where the rocks were actually close to the surface.

"Look at those two dark stones," he said, pointing. "At high tide you can barely see them. A ship that turns between them will break her hull."

Ned studied the place where the second light had appeared. There was no tower, only an abandoned signal station on the cliff and a narrow track leading down towards the sea.

"The old station closed after the telegraph cable was moved," Pike explained. "People say it is empty."

"People also said Samuel Vale fell by accident," Maggie answered.

Pike smiled sadly. "Then we had better look for ourselves."

They landed in a sheltered inlet and climbed a rough path. Rain had softened the ground, leaving clear marks from heavy boots. Near the station door, Ned found a deep circular mark in the earth, as if a large wooden pole had stood there.

Inside, the building smelled of damp stone and old smoke. A broken window faced the sea. On the floor were fresh candle wax, two short lengths of red-painted twine, and a piece of thin copper.

"Someone has been here recently," Maggie said.

Ned held the copper towards the light. One edge was smooth; the other showed marks where metal had been cut.

Captain Pike examined the window. "With a lamp and a shutter, someone could make a light appear to move. The sailors would believe it was a different signal."

There was also a narrow groove along a roof beam. A rope could pass over it to raise or lower a lantern without the person holding it standing near the edge of the cliff.

As they searched, footsteps sounded outside. Ned quickly pushed Maggie behind an old cabinet, while Pike stood openly by the door.

A broad man in a dark coat appeared. He carried a bundle of nets and looked surprised to see them.

"This place belongs to nobody," he said. "What are you looking for?"

"Only the view," Pike replied. "You are Silas Crowe, aren't you?"

The man nodded. Crowe was a boatman who sometimes repaired nets for the harbour. His eyes rested on the copper in Ned's hand.

"Storms throw strange things about," he said. "Be careful on the rocks."

He left without waiting for an answer. They watched him disappear down the path towards a small rowing boat.

"He didn't ask about the missing girl," Maggie observed.

"Nor about the dead keeper," Ned replied. "Perhaps he already knows."

Pike would not let them follow the boat. "The tide is turning," he warned. "We would lose the safe landing."

At the harbour, Ned drew the station in his notebook, marking the roof beam, broken window, and deep hole near the path. Maggie wrote down what Crowe had said, word for word.

That evening a boy named Alfie Bell delivered a package to the telegraph office. Inside was a small piece of blue cloth and a note in unfamiliar handwriting: STOP LOOKING AT THE WESTERN STATION.

Ned reached for the cloth. It was the same pale blue as the ribbon Clara usually tied around her notebook.

Maggie's voice shook. "If this is hers, someone wants us to know they have her."

Ned looked at the words again. Whoever had written the warning had made one mistake. The note confirmed that their questions were making someone nervous. Clara might still be alive. Their search had become dangerous, but it had also become necessary.

# CHAPTER 4 — THE MAN WITH THE RED ROPE

Constable Hale listened impatiently as Ned described the old station and the warning note. He agreed that someone had used the building but seemed more interested in the red thread.

"Rope is rope," he said. "Every boatman in Dunmere owns some."

"Then we should find out whose it was," Ned answered.

Hale sent an officer to question Thomas Baird, a carpenter who repaired the harbour posts. Baird used red-painted twine to identify tools belonging to his workshop. He was arrested briefly, and the news spread through the village by lunchtime.

Ned visited Baird's wife. She showed him the carpenter's receipts. On the night of the storm, Baird had been working in the inland village of Harrowford, repairing a bridge. Several workers could confirm it. The constable released him that evening.

"That is what happens when a clue becomes a conclusion," Dr Calder told Ned. "A red thread is only a red thread until you can explain where it came from."

Ned remembered the thick rope beneath Voss's desk. He decided to study the official harbour records again. A ship called the Mercy Dawn had passed Dunmere the week before Samuel died. Its cargo list claimed it carried ordinary timber. Yet the harbour account showed that the vessel had paid a large emergency salvage fee.

"How can a ship pay for a wreck that never happened?" Ned asked Maggie.

They checked the old newspaper reports. A small trading vessel had struck Raven's Point the previous spring. Its cargo had been recovered by a private company. Voss had signed the certificate giving that company permission to collect the goods.

The same company name appeared in three other damaged-shipping reports: West Coast Recovery.

Maggie examined the dates. "All these accidents happened on nights when there was poor visibility."

"And perhaps a false light," Ned added.

The company's office was registered in Plymouth, but its local agent was Silas Crowe. The papers did not prove murder. They did suggest that wrecked ships had become valuable business.

Near sunset Ned passed the harbourmaster's office and saw Voss speaking urgently to Crowe behind the closed shutters. He could not hear the words, but Crowe was holding a narrow metal box. When Ned moved closer, Voss noticed him and shut the window.

At home Ned copied the evidence into his notebook. He wrote three lines on separate pages: the red rope, the old signal station, and the salvage certificates. He drew arrows between them but left a blank space in the centre.

The blank space mattered. If Voss and Crowe were collecting cargo from wrecked ships, why had Samuel Vale become involved? Perhaps the keeper had noticed the false light and threatened to report it. But Ned could not explain why Clara had vanished.

He heard a quiet knock at his door. Captain Pike stood outside.

"There's something you should see," the fisherman said.

On his boat lay a rusted brass fitting. "I found it among my nets. It's a lamp bracket, made to hold a heavy lantern. Someone attached a new length of red rope to it recently."

"Where did you find it?"

"Below Raven's Point."

Ned placed the bracket beside the piece of copper from the station. Their holes matched.

For the first time, he knew the light had been built deliberately. What remained was to identify who had ordered it, who had carried it, and how that person had turned a deadly trick into a profitable business.

# CHAPTER 5 — CLARA'S SIGNAL

Maggie spent the next day studying the telegraph messages that had passed through Dunmere in the previous month. Most were ordinary: weather reports, shipping arrangements, news from relatives, and orders for supplies. But she noticed several messages addressed to West Coast Recovery. They were sent shortly after the nights when ships had struck the rocks.

One message used an unusual phrase: THE WINDOW IS OPEN. Another read: THE WINDOW IS CLOSED UNTIL MONDAY.

"Those are not shipping instructions," Maggie said. "They sound like a private code."

Ned compared the dates with the weather records. THE WINDOW IS OPEN appeared on nights with heavy cloud or fog. THE WINDOW IS CLOSED arrived when the sea was clear. Someone was choosing nights when sailors would have difficulty seeing the true coast.

Maggie tried to send a message to Clara's aunt in Plymouth. The local telegraph line stopped working halfway through the transmission. The instrument made a weak clicking sound and then fell silent.

"That cable was working this morning," she said.

They examined the outer wall. A wire had been cut in a place where a person could easily reach it from the yard. This was no accident.

Ned crossed the harbour to tell Captain Pike, who was repairing a sail. Suddenly Alfie Bell came running down the quay.

"A lady told me to give you this," he said.

He held out a small envelope. Inside was a strip of paper bearing three groups of pencil marks:

TWO SHORT. ONE LONG. THREE SHORT.

Below them was a single sentence: ASK WHO OWNED THE MERCY DAWN.

"Who was the lady?" Ned asked.

"She wore a scarf over her face. She was near the old fish market. When I turned back, she had gone."

Maggie joined them and studied the message. "Clara knows signals. Her father taught her the old lamp code. She might have written this herself."

"Why tell us to ask about the Mercy Dawn?"

"Because whoever owns it may own the answer."

Pike knew the ship. It had been sold twice during the past year. The current owner was not a sailor but a businessman whose name appeared on the documents through a holding company.

At the town registry, Ned discovered that the company was controlled by Stephen Voss. The harbourmaster had never mentioned owning any vessel.

That night, Ned walked to the house of Dr Calder. He showed her both messages and the record of the broken telegraph line.

"If Clara is alive, she may be held somewhere near the harbour," the doctor said. "But we must not assume every note is hers."

Ned agreed. The warning note could have been written by Crowe. The new message could be a trick. He needed a fact that no stranger could easily invent.

Maggie supplied it. "The pattern of signals is the order Clara and I used when we were children. We called it the stubborn seagull. Nobody else knows that name."

"Then the marks are hers."

Maggie nodded. "And she wants us to look at the Mercy Dawn."

At midnight the lantern at Raven's Point flashed again. This time it showed two short lights, then one long, then three short. Ned saw it from the harbour office window.

"Clara is alive," he whispered.

But the signal came from the place where the false light had first appeared. Either Clara was somewhere near the rocks, or her captors had learned her private code. Ned knew they could not wait for daylight to decide.

# CHAPTER 6 — BENEATH THE SIGNAL STATION

The tide would fall after midnight. Captain Pike chose the narrow inlet below Raven's Point, where rocks sheltered his boat from the worst waves. Ned and Maggie joined him, carrying one covered lantern and a coil of rope.

"We make no unnecessary noise," Pike said. "If there are people at the station, we leave and bring the police."

They climbed the familiar path. At the back of the ruined building, Ned noticed a line of damp marks beneath the stone wall. Water ran out through a narrow crack even though the rain had stopped. There had to be a hollow space inside.

The cellar door was hidden beneath a pile of rotten boards. Ned moved them aside. A staircase led down to an old storage room where signal oil had once been kept. The air smelled of salt and wet timber.

From behind another door came a faint tapping sound.

Maggie stopped walking. Two taps, a pause, one tap, then three.

"Clara?" she whispered.

The tapping repeated.

The door was locked. Captain Pike used an iron tool from his boat to force its old fastening. Inside, a young woman sat on the floor with her wrists tied loosely in front of her.

"Clara!"

Maggie knelt beside her. Clara's face was pale, and a bruise marked one side of her forehead. But she was awake.

"I knew you would understand the signal," she said.

Ned offered water. Clara drank slowly. "My father discovered that someone was showing false lights to trading ships. When the ships struck the rocks, the same men collected the cargo. He copied the names and dates."

"Did Stephen Voss know?"

Clara looked away. "He came to the lighthouse on the night of the storm. Father argued with him. I heard a heavy sound and ran into the passage. Then someone caught me."

"Was it Voss who took you?"

"No. Silas Crowe. He said Father had fallen and I must stay quiet until the storm was over. He brought me here by boat. I tried to escape, but the door was locked."

"Did you see who hurt your father?"

Clara shook her head. "I heard his voice. That is all."

She explained how she had found a loose stone beneath the outer wall and pushed a message through it. Alfie had collected the paper after seeing her hand through a gap. The second message was hers too. Crowe had left a box of matches and a narrow viewing hole. Clara had managed to flash a covered lamp in their childhood pattern.

"Where is the missing page from your father's record book?" Ned asked.

Clara's eyes filled with tears. "I tore it out when he told me to hide it. Then Crowe searched my coat. He took the page."

From above came the sound of a door opening.

Pike raised his lantern cover for an instant. A shadow moved across the cellar stairs.

"Ned Mercer," a voice called softly. "You should never have come down here."

It was Silas Crowe.

Pike pulled the storage door shut and dragged an old barrel against it. "The sea tunnel," he whispered.

Clara pointed towards a narrow passage that led to an emergency exit used by the former signalmen. Water splashed around their boots as they moved through the darkness.

Behind them Crowe shouted for help. The barrel crashed against the stone floor, but the four had already reached the lower opening.

They climbed into Pike's boat as the tide began to rise. A lantern moved above them, searching the rocks. Pike pushed away from the shore, using the current rather than the noisy oars.

Back in Dunmere, Dr Calder examined Clara. She would recover, but she needed rest. Her rescue answered one question and raised another: the keeper's daughter had heard a quarrel, but had not seen the fatal blow.

Before dawn, Clara asked Ned for his notebook. She drew a small shape beneath the old signal station: a square with an iron ring.

"That is where Crowe kept his locked box," she said. "I heard him open it every evening."

Ned closed the notebook. Somewhere inside that box might be the missing page—and the proof that Samuel Vale had been killed for discovering a crime.

# CHAPTER 7 — THE LEDGER OF WRECKS

By morning Stephen Voss knew Clara had escaped. He came to Dr Calder's house with Constable Hale and insisted that the girl be questioned immediately.

"She has been through a terrible night," the doctor said firmly. "You will wait until she is ready."

"Important property may have been stolen from the harbour office," Voss replied.

Ned noticed how carefully the harbourmaster avoided looking at Clara.

Maggie sent a new message from the western station, where the telegraph still worked. She contacted Inspector Alice Carver in Plymouth and explained that they had discovered a kidnapped woman and evidence of a false coastal light. The inspector promised to travel to Dunmere.

While they waited, Ned and Pike visited the abandoned station again. Crowe had gone, and the cellar door stood open. In a corner beneath a stone slab, Ned found a metal box with an iron ring exactly where Clara had described.

The lock had been broken. Inside were several wet receipts, a small notebook, and one torn page. On the page Samuel Vale had listed three ship names and four dates. Beside each date he had written the same words: SECOND LIGHT WEST OF RAVEN'S POINT.

The small notebook belonged to Crowe. It recorded payments for "night work" and the hours of certain high tides. Two entries ended with the initials S.V.

Pike frowned. "That could mean Stephen Voss. Or somebody else."

Ned agreed. He refused to call initials proof. He wanted to compare the payments with the official harbour ledger.

At the office, Voss was absent. His assistant, Mrs Ellen Ward, allowed Ned to see the records that were normally part of his work. He copied the entries for West Coast Recovery and the Mercy Dawn.

The numbers did not agree. Four missing cargo shipments appeared in the salvage ledger as recovered goods, yet the customs book showed they had already been unloaded before their ships were reported damaged. The "wrecks" were partly invented, allowing the company to claim fees for goods it had never rescued.

Maggie sat opposite Ned and checked the arithmetic. "Here, the same crates are counted twice. Once as a regular delivery, once as salvage."

"That explains the money," Ned said. "But Samuel might have discovered more. Perhaps some ships really were deliberately driven onto the rocks."

Mrs Ward searched an old file and found a letter sent by Samuel to the harbourmaster three weeks earlier. The keeper had demanded an independent investigation into several accidents.

"I placed this on Mr Voss's desk," she said. "He told me he had dealt with it."

The door opened. Voss entered and found them surrounded by books.

"You have no right to search my office!"

"These are public harbour records," Mrs Ward answered quietly. "And Ned has been checking them for years."

Voss seized the copied pages, but he could not remove the originals without being noticed. He turned to Ned.

"If you wish to keep your position, go home and forget this business."

Ned was frightened. He relied on the wages to support his widowed mother. Yet Clara had been locked in a cellar, and Samuel Vale would never return home.

"I cannot forget it," Ned said.

Voss's face hardened. "Then you no longer work here."

Outside, Ned told Maggie what had happened. She put a hand on his arm.

"You have lost your job because you would not ignore the truth," she said. "It may not feel brave, but it is."

That evening Dr Calder studied Samuel's wound again. She said it was consistent with a heavy round object, not necessarily a fall against an iron step. Pike immediately remembered the lead sounding weights kept in harbour offices and boats. They were used to measure the depth of the water.

Ned thought about the object Crowe had carried beneath his coat, the oil mark on Samuel's sleeve, and Voss's sudden anger. A weight might have been the weapon, but where was it now?

The answer came the next morning in a message from Inspector Carver: I ARRIVE AT FOUR. PRESERVE ALL DOCUMENTS. QUESTION NO ONE ALONE.

For the first time, the investigation had reached beyond the village.

# CHAPTER 8 — THE MESSAGE THAT VANISHED

Inspector Carver arrived on the afternoon train wearing a plain dark coat. She listened while Ned, Maggie, Clara, Pike, and Dr Calder told their stories. She asked the same question several times in different ways and wrote down every answer.

"You are not here to decide who is guilty," she told them. "You are here to tell me exactly what you know."

She studied the keeper's torn page and the payment notebook. Then she inspected the old signal station. Her officers found marks from the lamp bracket and a rope groove in the beam. They also collected the red twine, sealing it in a small envelope.

Carver asked Constable Hale to fetch the harbour ledger. Hale left with two men but returned without the book.

"The record room has been broken into," he said. "The ledger has vanished."

"Who had keys?" the inspector asked.

"Mr Voss, his assistant, and I."

Carver looked at him for a long moment. "Then we shall examine the locks and ask all three."

Ned did not like Hale's nervous expression. He had been quick to dismiss the death as an accident and had arrested Baird without checking his alibi.

At the western telegraph station, Maggie discovered a message sent from Dunmere just before the line was cut. Its words were short: THE PAPERS WILL BE MOVED AT LOW WATER.

"Who sent it?" Carver asked.

"The record shows an official station code," Maggie said. "But the handwriting on the instruction slip is not mine."

Constable Hale looked towards the floor.

Carver questioned him privately. Half an hour later, she told the others that Hale had admitted receiving money from Crowe in exchange for warnings about police searches. He claimed he knew nothing about Samuel's death. The inspector suspended him and placed him under guard.

"Even corrupt men can tell a useful truth," Carver said, "but every statement must be checked."

Hale had revealed that Crowe kept a second boat behind the old fish warehouse. At low tide, a narrow waterway connected the warehouse to Raven's Point.

The inspector organised a search. Ned was not allowed to join the officers, but he and Maggie helped explain the local tide table. If a boat tried to leave after the water began rising, it would be trapped between the channel walls.

While Carver's men searched the warehouse, Voss disappeared. His house was empty, and Mrs Ward said he had not returned to the office.

Ned felt a mixture of fear and relief. Voss's disappearance suggested that the investigation was finally close to him, but it also meant he had nothing left to lose.

Just before sunset Clara came to the telegraph office. She had remembered something important.

"When Father argued with the visitor, I heard him say, 'You signed it yourself, Stephen.' I thought he meant a letter. Now I think he meant the wreck certificates."

"Did he mention a weight?" Maggie asked.

Clara nodded slowly. "There was a loud metal sound, like the sounding lead dropping onto the floor."

Ned looked at Maggie. The lamp, the ledger, and the keeper's wound were beginning to form one story.

Then a note was pushed beneath the office door. No one had seen the messenger.

LEAVE THE RECORDS AT RAVEN'S POINT BEFORE MIDNIGHT OR THE HARBOUR WILL BURN.

Ned wanted to run outside, but Inspector Carver stopped him.

"A threat is meant to make us act without thinking," she said. "We will protect the harbour and we will not give them the records."

She placed officers at the warehouses and sent Pike to watch the narrow channel from his boat. The storm clouds returned, and the windows trembled in the wind.

For the second time that month, Dunmere prepared to face a light in the darkness. This time, the people waiting for it knew what it meant.

# CHAPTER 9 — THE LIGHT GOES OUT

At eleven thirty, Captain Pike spotted a low lantern moving near Raven's Point. Inspector Carver watched from the harbour road through borrowed binoculars. She had positioned officers along the safe paths and ordered them not to approach the edge in darkness.

Voss was standing near the old station. Crowe waited beside a covered cart. Two sacks lay in the cart, and one was tied with red twine.

Carver's officers moved quietly towards the building. A shout came from the path. Crowe turned, grabbed his lantern, and tried to escape towards the inlet. But the tide was rising, and Pike had already placed his boat across the narrow channel.

Voss remained at the station door. "You cannot arrest me on rumours!" he cried.

Carver approached with a lantern shielded from the wind. "We have records, witnesses, and your own shipping certificates. I suggest you come peacefully."

Voss raised his hands, but then suddenly pushed a wooden pole towards the roof beam. A lantern hanging from it swung out above the rocks, shining west across the sea. It was the false light.

"Put it out!" Pike shouted from below. "There is a vessel beyond the point!"

An approaching coastal steamer had seen the dangerous signal. Its course began to change.

Ned, watching from a safe place beside Maggie, realised the risk. He ran to the village bell, and Maggie sent an urgent warning to the western station. A line of lamps flashed from the safer coast, guiding the ship away from the rocks.

Above them, an officer cut the false lantern's rope. It fell into a sheltered pool and went dark.

Voss tried to run, but the inspector stopped him. Crowe was caught a few minutes later when his boat struck the bank and could not pass through the channel.

Inside the cart Carver found the missing harbour ledger and a heavy lead sounding weight. One side had been cleaned, but dark material remained in the narrow grooves near the metal ring.

Dr Calder would later compare the shape of the weight with Samuel's injury. It was consistent, although the inspector made clear that the object alone did not prove who had held it.

Crowe began to speak once he understood Voss would not protect him. He described the false-light scheme: a lantern on a movable pole, a shutter to change its appearance, and a hidden path used to collect cargo. Some damaged vessels had been deliberately misled; other supposed accidents were invented on paper.

"Who struck Samuel Vale?" Inspector Carver asked.

Crowe stared at Voss. "I heard the quarrel. Voss took a sounding weight from the table. Then I heard a crash. When I entered, the keeper was on the floor."

Voss said nothing.

"Why take Clara?" the inspector continued.

"I was told she had seen too much," Crowe answered. "Voss said she must be kept quiet until the papers were destroyed. I locked her in the old cellar. I never wanted her hurt."

"You did hurt her," Carver replied. "Keeping a frightened young woman imprisoned is not a small thing."

Maggie looked at Clara, who stood with Dr Calder at the top of the road. The girl had insisted on coming once the area was safe. She watched the officers lead Crowe and Voss away.

"I thought I might be too afraid to see them again," she whispered.

"You were afraid," Maggie answered. "And you still helped us find the truth."

Below the cliffs, the steamer sounded its horn. It passed safely into the eastern channel. The real lighthouse continued its steady work, and not another false light appeared that night.

# CHAPTER 10 — A TRUE LIGHT

The inquiry lasted several weeks. Inspector Carver collected the harbour records, statements from sailors, payment receipts, telegraph slips, and the sounding weight. She asked the survivors of two earlier wrecks to describe what they had seen on the coast.

The men remembered a light that stood too low and disappeared in a strange pattern. Their evidence matched Ned's drawing of the station and the mechanism recovered from Raven's Point.

Crowe admitted his part in the false-light operation and Clara's kidnapping. He also told the court that Voss had attacked Samuel Vale during their argument. The judge heard supporting evidence from Dr Calder, Clara, Mrs Ward, and several other witnesses.

Voss had controlled the harbour certificates, claimed salvage payments, and secretly owned the Mercy Dawn through another company. The records showed that he had benefited financially from wrecks he had helped to create. He denied intending to kill Samuel, but the court found him responsible for the keeper's death and the wider crimes.

Dunmere was shocked. People had trusted the harbourmaster for years. Some were angry with themselves for believing his official position made him honest. Others remembered that Captain Pike and Ned had questioned the strange light from the very first night.

Constable Hale faced separate charges for accepting money and warning the criminals. Thomas Baird received a public apology for the suspicion placed on him. Ned was offered his old job again, this time under a new harbourmaster who promised to let staff report irregularities without fear.

He accepted, but asked that the office records be checked by two people rather than one. The new harbourmaster agreed.

The first winter morning after the trial was cold and clear. Ned walked up the road to Blackglass Head carrying a new record book.

Clara was waiting at the lighthouse door. Her aunt had come from Plymouth to stay with her, and an experienced keeper had been appointed until she decided what she wished to do.

"I wanted you to have this," Ned said, handing her the book.

She opened the cover. On the first page he had written: FOR ALL THAT SAMUEL VALE SAW, AND ALL THAT HE TRIED TO PROTECT.

Clara smiled sadly. "Father would have told you to write the weather first. He believed every good record began with the sky."

Ned looked towards the horizon. "Clear. Light wind. Good visibility."

"Then write that," she said.

As he did, Captain Pike's fishing boat appeared below the cliff. Maggie stood on the harbour wall and waved a small handkerchief. She had restored the telegraph schedule and was training a young assistant, so that an emergency message would never again depend on one person.

Clara closed the book and looked at the lighthouse. She had once feared that she would never enter it again. Now she understood that the place held more than painful memories. It also held the work her father had loved.

"I may help the new keeper for a while," she said. "Then I want to learn more about navigation. I know the lamps and charts, but there is much I still don't understand."

"You helped us find a missing page, a hidden cellar, and an entire criminal business," Ned replied. "I think you understand quite a lot."

They laughed quietly.

Before leaving, Ned checked that the new record book lay safely on the desk. No pages were missing. Nothing had been hidden in the back drawer. Outside, the sea reflected the pale morning sun.

For a brief moment he remembered the false lantern swinging over Raven's Point. One dishonest light had almost destroyed several ships and many lives. Yet another light—the real one—had remained steady through every storm.

That was what Ned wanted for Dunmere: not a village without danger or mistakes, but a place where people could ask questions, speak truthfully, and help each other see the way.

When evening came, the lighthouse beam moved over the water again. Ships followed it towards the safe channel, and every sailor knew which light to trust.
